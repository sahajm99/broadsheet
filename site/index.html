A search engine built from scratch over — Financial Times articles from 1991, and then measured the way TREC measures search.

    From text to terms

    Before anything can be searched it has to be cut into terms. The course pipeline lowercases, splits on every non-alphanumeric character, throws away any token containing a digit, drops — of the remaining tokens as stopwords, and folds what is left with the Porter stemmer: — word forms become — stems.

    Each rule costs something. Throwing away tokens with digits removes prices, dates and share counts, which is a good part of what a financial newspaper prints. The stopword list takes out half the token stream, and with it any query that leans on function words. Stemming merges forms that mean the same thing, and a few that do not. What the rules buy is size and reach: shorter posting lists, a smaller index to ship, and a search for one form of a word that finds the others.

    Vocabulary keeps growing as text arrives, at roughly tokens—, and frequency falls away as roughly one over rank, with a fitted slope of —. The next two charts plot both on log axes, where a power law is a straight line.

    Neither fitted exponent is the one the textbooks quote. Vocabulary here grows faster than the usual square-root rule of thumb, because a month of newspaper copy keeps introducing names. Frequency falls away more slowly than one over rank. Both fits are straight lines drawn through curves that are not straight, so read them as a summary of this collection rather than as a law it obeys.

    The index

    The inverted index holds — postings over — stems, and — of those stems occur in exactly one article. The median article is — terms long once the stopwords are gone.

    Those two facts shape everything that follows. Nearly half the vocabulary is dead weight for retrieval, cheap to store and almost never asked for, while a handful of stems appear in thousands of articles and carry almost no information. And the articles themselves are short and much of a muchness in length, which is worth remembering when the length normalisation in BM25 is tuned later on.

    The heaviest stems are what a financial newspaper says every day. The longest posting list belongs to —, in — articles. The course stopword list keeps honorifics, so mr survives the filter and sits at the top of the frequency table, ahead of every word about money.

    Counting words says what the collection is made of. It says nothing about whether the engine puts the right article in front of a reader who asks a question, and that needs someone's opinion about which articles answer which questions.

    Does it find the right articles?

    Measuring a search engine takes three things: a set of questions, a ranking for each one, and a human judgment about which articles answer it. TREC supplies all three. Assessors at NIST read a pooled sample of what the search systems of the day returned for each topic and marked each article relevant or not. Those judgments were made over Financial Times files that run from 1991 to 1994, and only the 1991 files are here, so a topic whose relevant articles were printed in a later year cannot be answered from this collection at all.

    The NIST Robust 2004 judgments cover this collection, and filtered to the FT 1991 slice they leave — topics with at least one relevant article, out of — topics in all, with — relevant article-topic pairs across — judged articles. Topics with nothing relevant in the slice are dropped rather than scored as zero, because a zero there measures the slice and not the engine. Every mean below carries a percentile bootstrap interval over the topics that remain.

    Two conventions decide what those means are worth. An article no assessor ever looked at counts as not relevant, which is what TREC does, and which can only push a score down. So every number here is a lower bound. The second convention is to say how far down: the judged share of the top ten, the fraction of each ranking that any assessor saw at all, is about — for BM25. Roughly half of what the engine returns is being scored against a blank.

The log-tf variant, the usual textbook fix to the course weighting, lands at — on titles, slightly below the weighting it was meant to improve. A mean hides how uneven all of this is from topic to topic.

    The floor is crowded. A handful of topics score zero under every ranker, and — of the evaluable topics have exactly one relevant article, so their score is one divided by the rank of that article and nothing in between. Means over a distribution like that move for reasons that have little to do with the weighting, which is why the comparison is run topic by topic instead.

    Counting topics won and lost ignores where in the ranking the gain happened. A precision-recall curve asks that directly: as you walk down the list and pick up more of the relevant articles, what share of what you have read so far was worth reading.

    Two more questions the course version asked and never answered. The first is whether a longer query helps. Each TREC topic carries a short title, a sentence of description and a paragraph of narrative, so the same engine can be run on all three. Adding the description moves BM25's mean by —, with an interval that straddles zero. Adding the narrative as well moves it by —, and the lower bound of that interval sits a hair above zero, so it is compatible with no effect and must not be read as a clear win.

    The last question is what the text rules buy once retrieval is what you are measuring. Stemming moves the mean by — and removing stopwords by —, and both intervals include zero, so neither is separable from noise at this number of topics. That is not an argument for dropping them. They still shrink the vocabulary and the index by the amounts the first two sections measured, and they do it without costing anything the evaluation can detect.

    What changed since the course version

    The submitted project ran, but it was never scored. Its output carried internal identifiers instead of document numbers, so it could not be compared against a judgment file at all, and it weighted terms by raw count. Four things changed, and the — evaluable topics above are the result.

    • The run file carries document numbers. The course version wrote its own internal identifier for each article, which no scoring program can match against a judgment file. Writing the TREC document number instead is the change that made every number on this page possible.
    • Two more weightings sit beside the course's. A log-weighted term frequency and BM25 run against the same index as the submitted tf-idf cosine. The course weighting is kept exactly as it was, raw term count times inverse document frequency, so the comparison has a real baseline rather than a straw one.
    • The evaluation is actually run. Every ranker is scored over the — evaluable topics on average precision, precision in the top ten, nDCG, recall and the judged share, and every mean and every paired difference carries a percentile bootstrap interval over topics. The course reports described precision and recall without computing either.
    • One stemmer in two languages, both tested. The Porter stemmer is implemented in Python for the pipeline and in TypeScript for the browser, and both are checked word for word against Martin Porter's official vocabulary file. A second test checks the browser's top ten against the pipeline's for all — topic titles, so the search box above cannot quietly rank differently from the charts below it.

    The original course notebooks are kept unchanged under course/ in the repository, so the before and the after can be read side by side.

    What this cannot tell you

    Each of these applies to every number above it. The judgments see — of the — articles in this collection, and — of the evaluable topics have exactly one relevant article, so one ranking decision moves such a topic's score from zero to one.

    • This is a thin slice of the collection. The judgments were made over Financial Times files running from 1991 to 1994, and only the 1991 month is here. A topic answered entirely in another year scores zero, and that zero says nothing about the engine.
    • Unjudged counts as not relevant. An article no assessor opened is scored as a miss, which is the TREC convention and can only push a score down. Every mean here is a lower bound, and how loose a bound it is cannot be known from inside the data.
    • Many topics rest on a single article. For — of the evaluable topics, average precision is one divided by the rank of one article. Those scores jump in large steps, and a mean over them is noisier than its interval alone suggests.
    • The parameter grid is tuned on the test set. It was swept and scored on the same topics, so its best cell is the best fit to this slice. It is shown because the shape of the surface is informative, not because the winning cell is a setting to adopt.
    • A query is a bag of stems. There is no phrase matching, no proximity, no query expansion and no relevance feedback. One TREC topic asks about the British Chunnel, a word this newspaper never printed, because in 1991 it wrote "the Channel tunnel". That topic scores near zero, and no amount of reweighting would have helped it.
    • The stopword list is the course's. It is used unchanged, honorifics and all, because the subject of the rebuild is the course's pipeline. Nothing here tests whether a different list would rank better.
    • The Porter stemmer makes mistakes in both directions. It merges words that mean different things and leaves apart words that mean the same thing. It is the original algorithm, matched to its author's own test vectors, and its errors are inside every number above.
    • The subject is English financial news from 1991. Vocabulary growth, term frequencies and the value of stemming all depend on the kind of text being read. Nothing here transfers by default to another language, another domain or text written after the web.
    • No claim of statistical reliability is made. Every comparison is a mean with a percentile bootstrap interval over topics. Where an interval includes zero, or clears it by a hair, the honest reading is that the data does not separate the two, and the page says so in each place it happens.
    • This compares weightings, not engines. The question asked throughout is which term weighting finds more of the right articles inside one small engine. It is not a comparison against a modern retrieval system, and it should not be read as one.
